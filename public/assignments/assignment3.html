The Mammalian Circadian Clock

How does a cell keep time, and what happens to its clock when a gene is knocked out?

Before you begin

Individual mammalian cells can generate daily rhythms through feedback among clock genes and their protein products. In this assignment, you will reproduce the wild-type mouse circadian clock simulated by Mirsky et al. (2009), then use the same model to test which gene knockouts change or abolish its rhythm.

  • Download and read the paper. Focus on the regulatory network in Figure 1, the wild-type time courses in Figure 2, and the knockout analysis.
  • Use the Appendix for the 21 differential equations and the state definitions.
  • Use the Supporting Information for the parameter values (Table S1) and knockout conditions (Table S3). Figures S1 and S2 show selected knockout time courses.

The ODE model

The state vector contains eight mRNA concentrations, eight protein concentrations, and five protein complex concentrations. Time is measured in hours and concentrations are in nM. In the model, CLK/BMAL1 activates transcription of several clock genes. PER and CRY proteins form complexes that repress transcription at several of those genes. RORc and REV-ERBα provide additional regulation.

Wild-type clock

Problem 1: simulate the wild-type clock

Implement all 21 differential equations in Python using the parameter values in Table S1. Choose a nonnegative initial state and integrate the wild-type system.

Plot the eight mRNA and corresponding protein time courses in eight panels, following Figure 2. Use blue for mRNA and red for protein. Label time in hours and concentration in nM. Use separate, labeled concentration axes within a panel when the two traces are on very different scales. Show at least two complete cycles after the initial transient.

Problem 2: compare mRNA and protein peak times

Pick two mRNA–protein pairs and report the time lag from an mRNA peak to the corresponding protein peak for each pair. Compare your results with Figure 2 in the paper.

Gene knockouts

In this model, a knockout turns off transcription of the targeted gene. For each mutant, start from a fresh copy of the wild-type parameter set and set every transcription-rate coefficient listed for that mutant in Table S3 to zero. Keep the full system of equations so that any initially present target mRNA, protein, or complexes can evolve according to the model.

Problem 3: simulate the single and double knockouts

  1. Simulate all nine cases in Table S3. Include the seven single knockouts (Cry1, Cry2, Per1, Per2, Bmal1, Rev-erbα, and Rorc) and the two double knockouts (Cry1/Cry2 and Per1/Per2). List the coefficients you set to zero for each run. Plot the knockout time courses for at least two mRNAs whose genes are intact in that knockout. Include Per2 mRNA as one of the two whenever Per2 is intact. Show at least two cycles for every rhythmic knockout, including the slowest case. Include a plot of the initial transient if it helps distinguish damped oscillations from sustained oscillations.
  2. Make a summary table with one row per mutant. Classify each as rhythmic or arrhythmic, report the period for sustained rhythms, and describe changes in amplitude or late-time concentration relative to wild type. Explain the criterion you used to distinguish sustained rhythms from oscillations that decay, and extend the simulation if the classification is unclear. Compare each result with the simulated and experimental phenotypes in Table S3.

Graduate students only

Problem 4: explain what changes in the clock

  1. For each mutant, use Figure 1 and the equations to identify one regulatory interaction changed by the knockout. Connect the loss of a protein or complex to a transcription term in the model and explain how that change could contribute to the time course you observed.
  2. Compare the Cry1 and Cry2 knockouts. Identify which PER/CRY complexes each knockout removes and which remain. Use the Per2 transcription equation and the discussion accompanying Figure 4 to explain why these knockouts can produce different outcomes. Compare the Per2 mRNA amplitude in wild type and the Cry2 knockout.
  3. For the Rev-erbα and Rorc knockouts, identify a feedback path that remains intact. Explain how the model can retain a rhythm even when a regulatory interaction is lost.
